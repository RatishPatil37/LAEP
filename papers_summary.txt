ABSTRACT 
The Orbital High-Resolution Camera (OHRC), with a spatial 
resolution of 25cm, is one of  the most sophisticated optical 
sensors available for lunar exploration, capturing detailed 
observations of the lunar surface. This data is crucial for lunar 
missions to define safe and unsafe regions such as craters, 
ridges and boulders. The deep learning approach eliminates 
the need for manual intervention and streamlines the analysis 
of large lunar datasets. The combination of planetary data and 
modern processing techniques has significantly improved the 
accuracy and efficiency of crater detection, enabling detailed 
and large-scale analyses of planetary surfaces. The study area 
undertaken for the application of deep learning models for 
automated crater detection is the landing site of the NASA 
Apollo 12 (April 1967) and Surveyor 3 (November 1969) 
missions. Models such as YOLOv8 and Mask R-CNN were 
applied to detect medium to large-sized craters. The YOLOv8 
model result outperformed the Mask R-CNN result by 
providing higher accuracy and faster execution time. This 
study contributes valuable insigh ts to the planetary science 
community by highlighting the r obustness of different deep 
learning models for crater analysis using optical datasets from 
the Chandrayaan-2 lunar mission by ISRO, India. 
Keywords— OHRC, Chanrayaan-2, YOLOv8, Mask RCNN, 
Crater, Deep Learning 
1. INTRODUCTION 
The study of planetary science has reached new heights with 
recent technological advancements in remote sensing that 
have transformed our understanding of distant celestial 
bodies, enabling us to analyze surface composition, 
atmospheric dynamics, geographical features etc [1, 8, 9]. 
Planetary geographies, such as lunar terrain, are vastly 
covered with craters. The coll ision of extraterrestrial objects 
such as asteroids, comets, and meteorites on the lunar surface 
forms these circular surface depressions. These impact zones 
pose challenges for planetary miss ions in various domains, 
such as landing site selection of landers, navigation of 
extravehicular activities, rover p ath planning etc [2]. Crater 
detection is a formidable challenge due to their diverse and 
complex characteristics. The study focuses on the automated 
identification of lunar craters using the panchromatic dataset 
of the Chandrayaan-2 OH RC (Orbiter High-Resolution 
Camera) dataset, which provides a high spatial resolution of 
0.25m with a swath of 3km providing comprehensive insights 
into the lunar topography and morphology [3]. The area 
selected for this study cove rs the landing sites of NASA 
Apollo 12 (April 1967) /Surveyor 3 (1967) missions. A 
comparative analysis of modern  deep learning models such 
as YOLOv8 (You Only Look Once), which provides real-
time object detection capabilit ies, and Mask R-CNN (Mask 
Region-based Convolutional Neural Network), which uses 
pixel-level segmentation for object detection was performed 
[4, 5, 6]. This study demonstrates the improvements in 
accuracy and efficiency in identifying and analyzing intricate 
patterns within large planetar y datasets. Also highlights the 
potential to transform planetary d ata analysis, particularly in 
complex tasks such as object identification and classification.

4. METHODOLOGY 
The workflow for the study s tarts with acquiring data from 
the IRSO-ISDA portal for the lunar equatorial region. The 
single-band panchromatic data is pre-processed, which 
involves a sequence of steps such as distortion check, noise 
removal, etc. Data augmentation techniques such as rotation, 
scaling, and flipping are done to enhance the dataset’s 
diversity, thereby improving the model’s robustness. Data 
annotation is performed where lunar craters are labelled to 
create a ground truth dataset/mask for training the models.

The dataset is then split into training, testing and validation  
sets, with images resized to meet  model input requirements.
 
Deep learning models such as  YOLOv8 and Mask R-CNN 
were trained using the annotated data. During the training and 
testing, the hyperparameters of all the models wer e adjusted 
to achieve optimal performance . Lastly, the results are 
compared based on accuracy and computational efficiency to 
identify the best model fo r lunar crater detection and 
evaluation of the strengths and weaknesses of each model. 
 
4.2. YOLO v8 
 
YOLO is a state-of-the-art  object Convolutional Neural 
Network (CNN) for object detection. Its robust architecture 
makes it suitable for diverse co mputer vision-related tasks. 
Its architecture consists of a CSPDarknet backbone for 
efficient extraction of hierarchical features and a PANet and 
FPNet neck for merging the mu lti-scale features. And the 
head mechanism predicts bounding boxes for the features [2].  
 
4.3. Mask R-CNN 
 
Mask R-CNN is a deep learning model, an extension of Faster 
R-CNN that uses a predictio n mask for object detection 
parallel to bounding box recognition. The architecture 
comprises a ResNet backbone for feature extraction and 
RPNet (Region Proposal Netwo rk) for identifying object 
regions. The model gener ates bounding boxes, class labels 
and binary masks simultaneously using RoIAlign [5].

6. CONCLUSION 
 
The study represents an adva ncement in planetary science 
research, using modern deep learning models to automate the 
detection and segmentation of lunar craters using the 
Chandrayaan-2 OHRC data. The accuracy assessment shows 
that for crater identification, Ma sk R-CNN requires a large 
amount of labelled d ata for training to achieve accurate 
results. At the same time, the YOLOv8 successfully detected 
multiple craters without any notable difficulty and proved to 
be superior in handling the panchromatic data. Compared to 
Mask R-CNN, YOLOv8 requir es less computation time and 
generates a smaller size  model, making it ideal for real-time 
applications such as rover navigation, extravehicular 
activities etc., due to its lower time and space complexity.

Fengqi Zhang

Abstract—The craters widely distributed on the lunar surface
constitute important landforms for investigating the geological
activities and evolutionary history of the Moon. Currently, deep
learning models have made certain progress in detecting craters,
but they still have limitations in identifying numerous small
craters. To address these challenges, this article puts forward a
CenterNet-based dense small crater detection model (DSCrater-
Net), which is designed to precisely detect craters within the
permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) by leveraging the pre-
viously released enhanced PSR images. DSCraterNet achieves
precise crater localization by predicting keypoint heatmap
(location), size, and o ﬀset of craters. The model employs an
encoder–decoder network architecture that combines various fea-
ture fusion and attention mechanisms. Feature fusion strategies
include direct fusion between the encoder and decoder, multiscale
encoder spatial feature fusion (MSF) structure, and multielement
information fusion (MEF) structure. These fusion strategies
balance the relationship between high-resolution features, strong
semantic features, and multiscale spatial features according to
craters’ characteristics. Furthermore, by reasonably conﬁguring
attention-based layers [like e ﬃcient multiscale attention (EMA)]
at di ﬀerent stages, the utilization e ﬃciency of robust features
is improved. Ultimately, DSCraterNet successfully identiﬁed a
large number of densely packed small craters in PSR, revealing
the terrain features of the region. A series of thorough ablation
and comparative experiments has conﬁrmed that DSCraterNet
surpasses existing crater detection models in terms of precision,
recall, mean average precision (mAP), and F1-score metrics. Our
code is available at https: //github.com/dl-zfq/DSCraterNet

I. I NTRODUCTION
L
UNAR impact craters are circular depressions formed
by meteoroid collisions. Craters are the most visually
prominent landforms on the Moon. Studying craters not only
provides navigation guidance for lunar exploration equipment
[1] but also aids in advancing scientiﬁc missions related
to the lunar chronology, geological evolution, and resource
distribution [2]. The foundation and focus of crater research
is the comprehensive detection of craters and the creation and
updating of crater catalogs based on detected craters.
The lunar polar permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) are
the areas perpetually devoid of direct sunlight due to low solar
incidence angles. The potential water–ice and other crucial
resources in PSRs play a highly signiﬁcant role in providing
continuous support for future lunar scientiﬁc missions [3], [4].
Nevertheless, the widespread distribution of craters in these
areas leads to a substantial challenge for selecting landing sites
and conducting in situ exploration activities [5].
On the lunar surface like PSR, the craters display remark-
able diversity and variation in terms of size, shape, and
distribution pattern. Speciﬁcally, as shown in Fig. 1, small
craters are dominant in number and are extremely densely
distributed [6]. Many of these small craters are secondary
craters formed by debris from major impact events undergo-
ing subsequent impacts [7]. Additionally, some small craters
have undergone degradation and overlapping evolutionary
processes, which undoubtedly increase the complexity of
detection work. According to the conclusion from relevant
research [8], there are many small craters on the Moon, and
small craters a ﬀected by degradation are common. There-
fore, accurately detecting densely distributed small craters has
become a key challenge in detection missions.
Although most craters can be observed with the naked
eye, the expert observation method proposed by Robbins [8]
is time-consuming and labor-intensive, unsuitable for large-
scale, multiscenario lunar image data, and is limited to speciﬁc
geographic areas and large craters [9]. Moreover, the results
of crater detection after observation vary due to di ﬀerences
in subjective criteria and observation time. The di ﬀerence in
results from diﬀerent expert visual observations can even reach

Early researchers, based on the assumption that craters are
similar in shape to ellipses or circles [11], extracted custom
features represented by contours, shapes, edges, or depres-
sions, and used these features to capture craters [12], [13].
Hanak [14] proposed an extended ﬁlter for crater detection
based on the circular Hough transform. Kang et al. [15]
introduced a histogram of oriented gradient (HOG) feature
and support vector machine (SVM) classiﬁer when detecting
craters using CCD images. Chen et al. [16] used terrain
analysis and mathematical morphology methods to identify the
boundaries of craters. Wang et al. [17] obtained the features
of craters under various lighting conditions and then used the
isolation forest algorithm to eliminate false craters. However,
these traditional methods are limited by the obvious diameter
and morphological di ﬀerences of craters [18] and are not
suitable for detecting small craters in complex terrain and
varying lighting conditions [19], [20].
Compared to traditional learning methods, deep learning
methods based on convolutional neural network (CNN) can
eﬀectively address issues caused by variations in crater mor-
phology through model design. Wang et al. [7] and Jia et
al. [21] have implemented automatic crater detection based
on an improved U-Net network. Vellas et al. [22] tested
the use of various YOLO networks for real-time detec-
tion of Martian craters. Wang et al. [23] used YOLOv7
to detect craters in synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images
[24]. CraterIDNet [25] utilized a crater detection pipeline and
a crater identiﬁcation pipeline to obtain size and location
information of craters. ADSNet [26] extracted crater fea-
tures based on multimodal fusion. Some studies are based
on semantic segmentation to achieve crater detection. For
example, Lee and Hogan [27] used the ResUNET network
to process craters in digital terrain model data and optical
imaging data. Mao et al. [28] proposed an advanced dual-
path CNN to fuse crater features from a digital elevation
map (DEM) or orthographic projection map [wide angle
camera (W AC)] data. Sinha et al. [29] combined the U-Net
semantic segmentation architecture with the ResNet-18 feature
extraction backbone to detect craters. Emami et al. [30]
implemented unsupervised crater detection algorithms based
on classiﬁcation. These models have not been speciﬁcally
designed to extract features suitable for the characteristics
of the lunar surface, which is abundant in densely packed
small craters, leading to a large number of missed detections
in detection results. In addition, some recent hyperspectral
anomaly detection methods [31], [32] have also provided new
ideas such as self-supervision and integrated tasks for crater
detection.
Currently, CNN-based deep learning models struggle to
address three key challenges in PSR crater detection.
1) A high proportion of small craters within the lunar sur-
face craters. Due to the small diameter of small craters,
their feature expression is weak, and the erosion of
craters by weathering e ﬀects, as well as the overlapping
of some craters [33], collectively increase the di ﬃculty
of the model in identifying small craters. Small craters
and large craters generally refer to craters less than
1 km and greater than 10 km in diameter. However,
due to di ﬀerences in image resolutions, the deﬁnition
criterion for large and small craters in this article is the
bounding box size (30 and 200 pixels).
2) Extreme scale variance among craters, with scale di ﬀer-
ences causing certain models to miss very large or very
small craters during detection.
3) Craters appear in groups in some regions, resulting in
an increase in the missed detection rate. To address
these challenges, this article proposes an anchor-free
model called CenterNet-based dense small crater detec-
tion model, which outputs crater heatmap, size, and
oﬀset information based on the principles of CenterNet
[34]. DSCraterNet uses PSR images processed by the
enhanced model ZSPL-PSR [35], taking a connected
encoder–decoder as its basic architecture, and incorpo-
rates a feature pyramid network (FPN)-based [36] fusion
structure within the encoder to encode multiscale spatial
information, e ﬀectively capturing craters of di ﬀerent
scales. By fusing shallow high-resolution features, deep
strong semantic features, and multiscale spatial infor-
mation before the prediction head, and using a channel
attention structure to adjust their relative importance,
it achieves complete detection of PSR surface craters
dominated by small craters. The main contributions of
this work are as follows.
a) Inspired by CenterNet, we propose an anchor-free
model called DSCraterNet for PSR crater detec-
tion, where most small craters in PSR are detected
by the model output, using enhanced optical image
data.
b) DSCraterNet integrates encoder feature pyramid
fusion to obtain multiscale information, incorpo-
rates image and shallow feature maps to obtain
high-resolution information, and integrates the
feature maps output by the decoder to obtain
strong semantic information. In addition, the model
introduces corresponding attention mechanisms in
multiple parts to enhance feature learning capabili-
ties, thereby achieving eﬃcient prediction of dense
small craters (i.e., densely distributed craters with
diameters under 30 pixels).
c) Extensive experiments show that DSCraterNet
achieves an F1-score of 0.8 and mean aver-
age precision (mAP) of 74.04% on the test set,

II. M ETHODOLOGY
A. Related Work
Anchor-based object detection methods typically generate
a batch of candidate boxes through sliding windows or dense
sampling. However, the inherent characteristics of overlapping
and small-sized craters conﬂict with this mechanism. The
CenterNet-based approach proposed by Zhang et al. [37]
and Juntao et al. [38] directly predicts the key points of
objects, which has advantages in identifying dense and par-
tially occluded objects and is faster and more memory-saving
than anchor-based models. However, their model [37] uses
two consecutive Hourglass networks as the backbone, which
deepens the network and results in a quite large receptive ﬁeld
for the ﬁnal feature map, thus only providing an advantage
for detecting larger craters. Moreover, the output heatmap,
size, and o ﬀset are 1/R (R = 4) of the input image, and this
reduction in size is not conducive to restoring high-resolution
information, making the model challenging when detecting
small-sized and weakly featured craters. Additionally, the
complexity of this backbone network leads to slower operation
speed.
B. Proposed Method: DSCraterNet
To address the limitations of existing keypoint-based detec-
tors for small, dense craters, particularly concerning output
resolution and computational eﬃciency, we propose DSCrater-
Net, a novel anchor-free model based on the CenterNet
framework. As shown in Fig. 2, DSCraterNet consists of
a backbone network based on an encoder–decoder network,
a neck network that dynamically fuses high-resolution fea-
tures, multiscale features, and deep semantic features, and a
detection head that outputs information about detected craters.
Assuming that the input size is reduced to W × H × 3
(diﬀerent from the original image size) to accelerate training
speed, the sizes of the feature maps for the model’s output
keypoint Gaussian heatmap H, crater size S , and o ﬀset O

Note that the width and height of the predicted information

1) Backbone: The backbone network adopted by DSCrater-
Net is an encoder–decoder network. The function of the
encoder is to project image features onto low-dimensional
feature maps, thereby reducing the model complexity and
expediting the training process. The decoder conducts upsam-
pling operations on the low-dimensional feature maps through
transposed convolution (i.e., deconvolution), guaranteeing that
the upsampled feature maps are consistent with the dimensions
of the feature maps at various levels in the encoder. Transposed
convolution adaptively restores high-frequency details through
learnable parameters, which is more suitable for small target
edge reconstruction than ﬁxed interpolation methods. For
example, on the edge of a crater, deconvolution can produce
sharp contours, while interpolation can cause blurring. Then,
by concatenating the symmetric encoder and decoder feature
maps layer by layer, eﬀective fusion of shallow spatial features
and deep semantic features is achieved. Each fused feature
map is processed by an e ﬃcient multiscale attention (EMA)
module [39] to enhance the fusion.
Set the input image as I, and then, I∈ RW×H×3. I ﬁrst goes
through a preprocessing block PRE, which includes a convolu-
tion block COV and a residual block RES. COV consists of a
convolutional layer with a (3, 3) kernel size, padding of 1, 64
kernels, and a stride of 1, followed by a batch normalization
(BN) layer and a Relu activation function. RES adds the
input feature map to a feature map that has undergone two
identical 3 × 3 convolutions. The preprocessed feature map
Fpre is passed into the backbone based on an encoder–decoder
architecture after going through one RES. The encoder con-
tains four encoder layers, each with a downsampling block
DOW and the RES block, achieving four downsampling
eﬀects. RES blocks in four encoder layers change the number
of feature map channels to (64 → 128), (128 → 256),
(256→ 512), and (512 → 1024). The downsampling block
DOW is a COV block with a stride of 2. After four downsam-
pling operations, the dimensions of the feature map change
as follows:{(128, C/2, H/2), (256, C/4, H/4), (512, C/8, H/8),
(1024, C/16, H/16)}. The last layer is the bottleneck feature
map Fb between the encoder and the decoder. Then, it sequen-
tially goes through four decoder layers, each with a transposed
convolution block TCOV , an RES, and an EMA module.
TCOV upsamples the feature map through deconvolution, with
a (2, 2) kernel size and a stride of 2. The upsampled feature
map is then concatenated with the corresponding size feature
map from the encoder to achieve the fusion of shallow and
deep features. The EMA module is used to retain the key
information of the fused features. Finally, decoder outputs
the feature map F, where F∈ RW×H×64. The corresponding
layers’ fused features of the encoder and decoder are rich
in channel and spatial information. DSCraterNet employs
an EMA module to preserve key channel information and
reduce computational overhead. The EMA framework diagram
is shown in Fig. 3. It can be seen that after grouping the
feature maps by channels (the grouping factor is 32), 1-D
horizontal pooling, 1-D vertical pooling, and 3 × 3 convo-
lution are performed on the feature maps in three branches,
embedding channel information and multidirectional, multi-
scale spatial information. Moreover, by multiplying feature
maps of the pooling branch and the convolution branch, the
context information of pixels is captured, eliminating short-
range and long-range dependencies. Noteworthily, considering
the e ﬃciency, the ability to achieve larger receptive ﬁelds
through depth stacking, the suitability for capturing ﬁne-
grained features of small, densely packed craters, and the
synergistic eﬀect with the integrated MSF and attention mech-
anisms [EMA, SE, and spatial attention (SA)], we primarily
employ 3 × 3 convolution kernels throughout the DSCraterNet
architecture.
The backbone’s architecture achieves eﬀective extraction of
dense small targets and multiscale target information while
maintaining low computational costs.

is robust to changes in crater size.
3) Neck: Given the high proportion and density of small
craters, it is essential to preserve high-resolution information
during the process of feature extraction in DSCraterNet. In
addition to numerous small craters, the lunar surface also
contains some large craters, including super-large craters with
diameters much greater than those of small craters. Due
to the e ﬀects of secondary impacts, some large craters are
also covered with small craters. In light of this distribution
pattern, multiscale information also needs to be utilized.
To enhance the model’s ability to recognize the semantic
of craters, deep features also need to be involved in the
generation of crater regression information. Considering these
diﬀerent factors, the model implements the integration of
several types of information in the neck network following
the backbone. Therefore, this module is also called MEF
module.

III. E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND ANALYSIS
In this section, a series of comparative and validation
experiments is carried out to illustrate the advantages of the
model design in lunar crater detection.
A. Experimental Data
1) Dataset: In this article, the model is trained and tested
using a dataset created by CraterDANet [42]. The images used
in the dataset were captured by the Narrow Angle Camera
(NAC) on board the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO)
launched by NASA in 2009. The NAC images have a resolu-
tion of 0.5–2 m, making them the highest resolution optical
images of the Moon currently available. The high resolution
allows the images to contain a large number of small-diameter
craters. The dataset comprises 20 images, containing more
than 20 000 craters whose diameters range from 12 to 400 m.
The process of creating the dataset employed an unsupervised
strategy for annotation. We select eight images and two images
of size 800 × 800 and their annotations as the training set
and test set, respectively, to shorten the experiment time. The
corresponding numbers of craters in the training set and test set
are 10 253 and 2930, respectively, with the smallest bounding
rectangle box side length (size) being 7 and the largest being
258.
2) Enhanced PSR Image Data: Our previously released
zero-shot image enhancement model ZSPL-PSR has automat-
ically enhanced the PSR images located at the lunar poles.
Due to the lack of direct sunlight, the visibility of PSR images
is poor and much content is obscured. The enhanced images
fully display the internal topography. PSR is a key area for
studying the lunar surface landform and material composition.
As shown in Fig. 5, the enhanced images to some extent unveil
the mysterious veil of PSR, revealing numerous surface details,
including craters. Crater detection on such images is beneﬁcial
for exploring important resources such as water–ice in the
lunar polar regions.
B. Experimental Instructions
1) Experimental Details: The experiment is run on a Linux
server equipped with eight NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090
GPUs. All related experiments are programmed using the
Pytorch framework. The training process employs distributed
training to accelerate speed.
For all experiments based on CenterNet covered in this
section, the input images are resized to (512, 512) before being
fed into the model. The model utilizes the Adam optimizer
and employs a cosine annealing strategy to adjust the learning
rate. The initial learning rate is set to 5e −4, the minimum
learning rate to 5e−6, the batch size to 12, and the number of
epochs to 100. Data augmentation is achieved during training
by randomly ﬂipping images horizontally and varying the
hue, saturation, and brightness. Speciﬁcally, if the random

D. Ablation Study on Convolution Kernel Sizes
The size of the convolution kernel uniformly used in
DSCraterNet is 3 × 3. To verify this setting, this section
conducts experiments with various kernel combinations (3 ×
3, 5 × 5, and 7 × 7). Several di ﬀerent convolution kernel
combination schemes designed are denoted “Var1,” “Var2,”
“Var3,” and “Var4.” Var1 means replacing the original 3 ×
3 convolution kernel with a larger convolution kernel in the
ﬁrst two layers of the encoder (i.e., mixed use of 5 × 5 and
7 × 7 convolution kernels). Var2–Var4 represent that at the
bottleneck level, the MSF’s convolution layer and the MEF’s
(Neck) convolution layer implement this replacement process,
respectively.
It can be seen from Table II that the existing model
outperforms Var1–Var4 in four indicators. Crucially, these
larger kernels invariably resulted in a notable increase in
model size and inference time. This observation validates our
design choice. The stacked 3 × 3 convolutions in the deep
encoder–decoder backbone, coupled with the dedicated MSF
module, already e ﬀectively capture features across di ﬀerent
scales relevant to craters ranging from small to large. Intro-
ducing larger kernels introduces unnecessary computational
overhead without commensurate performance gains, particu-
larly detrimental to the detection of densely packed small
craters where ﬁne-grained details are paramount. Furthermore,
the eﬃciency of 3 × 3 convolutions aligns well with the goal
of maintaining a lightweight yet e ﬀective model. Var1’s worst
performance also proves that large convolution kernels destroy
the ﬁne spatial information that helps capture small craters.

G. Comparative Analysis
CenterNet is a keypoint-based single-stage end-to-end
detection model, and the principle of keypoint makes such
models more advantageous in detecting densely distributed
targets compared to two-stage anchor-based models. We com-

the e ﬃciency of the model design. These networks include
RseNet50, Hourglass, ResNet50 pretrained on the ImageNet
dataset, and improved U-Net, where Hourglass and improved
U-Net refer to the crater detection model proposed by
Juntao et al. [38] and Sinha et al. [29], respectively. Addi-

I. Limitations, Failure Cases, and Generalization
While DSCraterNet demonstrates noticeable advancements
in detecting densely packed small craters within lunar PSRs,
it is essential to acknowledge its limitations and potential
failure cases to provide a comprehensive understanding of its
applicability.
1) Severely Degraded or Occluded Craters: The model’s
performance may degrade for craters that are heavily
degraded by subsequent impacts, buried by regolith,
or partially obscured by larger overlapping craters or
terrain features. In such cases, the characteristic circu-
lar/elliptical rim signature and depression morphology
are often incomplete or ambiguous [see Fig. 11(a)].
DSCraterNet, primarily learning from visible rim struc-
tures and center-point keypoints, may fail to recognize
these fragmented or obscured features, leading to missed
detections (false negatives).
2) Noncircular/Nonelliptical Craters: The training data and
the implicit bias of the model (based on keypoint
detection of centers) primarily consist of craters with
approximately circular or elliptical shapes, which are
the dominant forms. However, some impact craters,
particularly secondary craters formed at oblique angles
or on steep slopes, can exhibit highly irregular or
elongated shapes. DSCraterNe’s reliance on predicting a
single center point and a bounding box (approximating
an ellipse) might struggle to accurately localize and
represent these atypical forms, potentially resulting in
missed detections or inaccurate size estimations [see Fig.
11(b)].
3) Regions of Exceptionally Low Contrast: Although the
ZSPL-PSR enhancement signiﬁcantly improves the vis-
ibility of PSR terrain, some areas within PSRs may
still exhibit extremely low inherent contrast due to fac-
tors like homogeneous regolith composition, extremely
shallow slopes, or sensor limitations. In such regions,
even the enhanced image may lack su ﬃcient textural
or intensity gradients for the model to discern subtle
crater features reliably [see Fig. 11(c)]. This represents
a fundamental challenge tied to the inherent information
content of the input data.
4) V ery Large Craters: As shown in Fig. 11(d), the very
few large craters in the images are not detected. This
is because the number of small craters far exceeds that

could potentially impact performance. Fine-tuning on
representative data from new terrains might be necessary
for optimal results.

optimized for the speciﬁc and challenging task of detecting
relatively well-preserved small-to-medium craters within lunar
PSRs using enhanced NAC imagery. Performance degradation
can be expected in the aforementioned edge cases. Future
work will focus on: 1) incorporating more sophisticated shape
modeling beyond bounding boxes (e.g., instance segmenta-
tion) to better handle irregular craters; 2) exploring domain
adaptation or few-shot learning techniques to improve gen-
eralization across diverse terrains and lighting conditions;
3) integrating multisource data (e.g., topography from DEMs)
as complementary inputs to overcome limitations in low-
contrast optical data; and 4) developing uniﬁed frameworks or
transfer learning strategies for cross-planetary crater detection.
IV. C ONCLUSION
In this article, according to the characteristics of dense
distribution, numerous small craters, and large-scale di ﬀer-
ences about lunar craters, we propose a single-stage end-to-end
crater detection model based on keypoint estimation, named
DSCraterNet, and apply it to crater detection in PSR to
facilitate the exploration of this unknown area. DSCraterNet
is based on an encoder–decoder network architecture and has
several advantages in detecting densely packed small craters.
1) The connections between the encoder and decoder layers
promote the fusion of deep features with complementary
spatial details.
2) The EMA attention layers following each layer of the
decoder retain the key channels and spatial information
of feature maps.
3) The multilayer feature maps of the encoder are com-
bined into multiscale spatial fusion features in an
FPN-like form.
4) The fusion of multiscale features from encoder, high-
resolution features from the model’s beginning, and
strong semantic features from the neck’s end, along
with the channel attention weighting process, enable the
model to obtain the ability to detect craters of various
shapes. These designs have led to superior performance
in extensive experimental tests and have detected a
large number of small craters based on enhanced PSR
images. This provides data support for size-frequency
distribution analysis of craters to study the geological
evolution and material composition of polar PSR.
In future work, we will conduct subsequent scientiﬁc
research based on the PSR’s crater detection results and
explore the continued use of deep learning methods to study
other landforms or surface materials within PSR.

IV . M ETHODOLOGY
A. YOLOv8m Architecture
YOLO, or Y ou Only Look Once, is a go-to option
for real time object detection and image segmentation.
YOLO is a convolutional neural network that predicts
both the bounding boxes and class probabilities of
the objects in an image in one pass. Here, we are
using the YOLOv8m model variant because of an
acceptable balance in computational efﬁciency and
detection accuracy for lunar crater detection. YOLOs
architecture has several main parts that it uses to
process data:
• Backbone: A modiﬁed CSPDarknet53 is used as
the backbone, which provides multi-scale features
using Cross Stage Partial (CSP) links to reduce
the parameters while keeping the feature space
expressive. The Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast
(SPPF) module aggregates features across scales,
enhancing detection of small and large craters [4].
• Neck: The Path Aggregation Network (PANet)
takes features from various layers in the backbone
to integrate spatial information to improve the
localization for craters of different sizes. The
PANet uses Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs) to
propagate feature information down from higher
resolution feature maps to the lowest resolution
feature maps.
• Head: The decoupled head separates the clas-
siﬁcation, regression and objectness predic-
tions. YOLOv8m’s anchor-free approach predicts
bounding box centers directly, reducing training
complexity and improving performance on irreg-
ular crater shapes [4].
The model accepts input images with a resolution of
1024x1024 pixels and outputs bounding boxes, class
probabilities, and conﬁdence scores of craters detected.
B. Loss Functions

D. Training and Hyperparameter Tuning
The baseline model was ﬁrst trained for 200 epochs
using the default hyper-parameters with the Ultralytics
YOLOv8 framework. In training, the images used
were of size 640x640 pixels, which were resized to
1024!1024 pixels using batch size of 16. This setup
gained 0.89 precision and a recall of 0.87.
To improve model performance, hyperparameter
tuning and data augmentation techniques were applied.
The initial learning rate (lr0) was set as 0.0005, the
learning rate factor (lrf) to 0.01, momentum to 0.937,
and weight decay to 0.0006. A warm-up strategy in-
cluded 3 warm-up epochs, where warm-up momentum
was 0.8, and the warm-up bias learning rate was 0.1.
Learning rate decay was used to help convergence and
generalization.
After these changes, the model was trained for
an additional 200 epochs, bringing the total to 400
epochs. These optimizations improved all key perfor-
mance metrics, with the ﬁnal model reaching 0.93
precision and recall of 0.91, showing better detection
capability and robustness.
To further enhance generalization to real lunar ter-
rain, the model was ﬁne-tuned on a manually annotated
subset of 1024 !1024 patches taken from a high-

sized at 1200 !9014 pixels. This domain-speciﬁc ﬁne-

contrast craters and adjust to different crater shapes.

ABSTRACT 
Permanently shaded areas located in close proximity to the 
lunar poles represent regions of extreme coldness that remain 
perpetually devoid of sunlight owing to the specific 
orientation of the Moon's axis. These regions, which could 
contain significant amounts of water-ice, are crucial for 
future lunar exploration. The cold temperatures in PSRs 
allow for the freezing and trapping of volatiles, including 
water ice. Numerous studies have aimed to detect water ice 
in these areas, such as the Chandrayaan-1 mission, which 
mapped the lunar surface and sought water deposits near the 
poles. This paper utilizes Moon Mineralogy Mapper data 
onboard Chandrayaan 1 and Mini-SAR instruments to detect 
water ice in and around the Erlanger crater situated at the 
lunar North Pole. The M3 hyperspectral images analyzed 
water ice absorption features, while image processing 
techniques and the Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) were used 
for mapping water ice deposits. Moreover, the application of 
Mini-SAR data aided in the measurement of Stokes 
parameters, which were subsequently utilized in 
decomposition techniques to differentiate scattering events 
inside and outside the crater. 
Index Terms— Permanently shadowed regions, Moon 
Mineralogy Mapper, Mini-SAR, Spectral Angle Mapper, 
decomposition techniques. 
 
INTRODUCTION 
Permanently shaded areas on the lunar surface remain devoid 
of direct sunlight as a result of the Moon's axis exhibiting close 
to perpendicular alignment with the sun's rays. This results in 
extremely cold temperatures (below 120 K) in these regions, 
allowing them to trap volatile materials. These distinctive 
characteristics make PSRs crucial for studying the Moon’s 
geology and potential resources[1].  
The issue of the existence of H 2O and other volatiles on the 
Moon is crucial in terms of science and exploration[2]. 
Water molecules can be found on the lunar surface due to a 
variety of processes, such as collisions from comets, asteroids, 
and meteorites, as well as reactions with protons from the solar 
wind and endogenous water. Understanding cometary activity 
at the lunar pole sheds light on the sources and fluxes of 
impacting materials over time [2]. Planetary exploration is 
crucial for comprehending celestial bodies' formation, 
evolution, and current geological state [3]. In order to identify 
regions with high scientific potential for studying volatiles, 
especially water molecule in permanently shadowed regions 
(PSRs) near the lunar poles, studies make use of data from 
missions like Chandrayaan-1 and instruments like the Lunar 
Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) and the Lunar Prospector 
Neutron Spectrometer (LPNS)[4] [5]. 
Chandrayaan-1 was launched to map the lunar surface and 
detect water ice deposits near the lunar poles. Through 
spectrum absorption features, multi and hyperspectral data in 
the visible, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave infrared 
regions are used in lunar remote sensing to analyze the Moon's 
minerals. This includes electronic absorption in the UV, 
visible, and NIR regions, and vibrational absorption from 
visible to NIR (1-30 micrometers). NIR reflectance 
spectroscopy is particularly effective for distinguishing water 
ice from hydroxyl (OH-) and OH-bearing minerals. It is an 
imaging spectrometer, provides high-resolution NIR 
reflectance data, allowing detailed analysis of the lunar 
surface, including its polar regions.[6]. 
This study focuses on detecting H 2O ice by examining 
diagnostic absorption depth near 1.5 and 2.9 μm using M3 
data from the Erlanger crater. The collected spectra were 
compared to reference water ice spectra, and the Spectral 
Angle Mapper (SAM) was used to map water ice pixels. 
Additionally, thermal infrared and microwave data were 
utilized to obtain information on the physical characteristics 
and chemical composition of the lunar surface and subsurface. 
The most common type of radar-based system for imaging is 
SAR, which also includes altimeters and scatterometers [7].  
The freshness of this work is that indirect light has been used 
to examine the spectra of permanently shaded regions and 
with these studies the characteristic absorption depth has been 
observed and using Mini-SAR data different decomposition 
model has been carried out for comparison with other models.

CONCLUSION  
This study utilized Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3) onb oard 
Chandrayaan 1 and Mini-SAR data to detect the prese nce of 
water ice in the Erlanger crater. The analysis did not consider 
the 3μm absorption depth due to the wavelength range of M3 
data, instead relying on the left shoulder of the 3 μm 
absorption feature and Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) 
classification to map water ice pixels. Three decom position 
models ((m - δ), (m - χ), and (m - α)) were employe d to 
retrieve and compare backscattering information fro m the 
lunar surface, revealing that the majority of the s urface 
experienced double bounce scattering, while the int erior of 
the crater showed volumetric scattering indicative of lunar 
regolith and water ice. High Circular Polarization Ratio 
(CPR) values and significant volumetric scattering inside the 
crater, coupled with low radar returns in permanent ly 
shadowed regions, suggest favourable circumstances for 
water ice to exist in the Erlanger Crater.

Abstract— Crater and boulder detection on the lunar surface 
is essential for understanding the Moon’s geological history, 
impact dynamics, and surface evolution. Traditional methods 
rely on manual annotation and classical computer vision 
techniques, which are often time-consuming, error-prone, and 
limited in their ability to detect small-scale features. The use of 
Orbiter High -Resolution Camera (OHRC) imagery with 
advanced computer vision techniques enhances detection 
accuracy, improving the reliability of crater and boulder 
detection. This paper introduces a novel crater and boulder 
detection approach utilising the recent YOLOv11 object 
detection model. The proposed detection approach is examined 
against the RE-DETR model to assess its effectiveness in 
accurately identifying craters and boulders. The dataset used to 
evaluate the proposed approach is the OHRC images from the 
Bharatiya Antariksh Hackathon 2024 by ISRO. The evaluation 
metrics used to assess the performance are precision, recall, 
𝐦𝐀𝐏𝟓𝟎  and 𝐦𝐀𝐏𝟓𝟎#𝟗𝟓 . The experimental results during the 
training phase show that precision, recall, 𝐦𝐀𝐏𝟓𝟎  and 
𝐦𝐀𝐏𝟓𝟎#𝟗𝟓  are increased by 4.8%, 2.4%, 5.0%, 7.9%, 
respectively compared to RE-DETR-based crater and boulder 
detection. Thus, YOLOv11-based crater and boulder detection 
outperforms RE -DETR-based crater and boulder detection.  
The proposed approach using the YOLOv11 model achieved 
75.9% accuracy during the testing phase.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Moon, the natural satellite of Earth, has been revolving 
around our planet for 4.51 billion years. During that period, a 
lot of geological changes happened on the moon. Two main 
changes have happened on the Moon: high -speed impacts 
from celestial bodies, and rock fracturing caused by geological 
forces, followed by weathering and erosion  [1]. High-speed 
impact of celestial bodies forms craters . Rock fracturing by 
geological forces, followed by weathering and erosion, breaks 
down larger rock into smaller pieces, which form  boulders. 
One of the most advanced imaging instruments developed by 
ISRO is the OHRC, which can capture the lunar surface in 
exceptional detail. OHRC image helps researchers detect fine-
scale features that were previously invisible using lower -
resolution devices. 
Boulders and craters provide important insights about the 
Moon's development and geological past. These findings 
depend on chemical analysis or high -resolution imaging, 
which are impossible to accomplish manually because of the 
vast distance. Rather, probes are dispatched with sophisticated 
analytical tools. The ability of remote sensing equipment 
installed on orbiters to record large surface regions in fine 
detail enables ongoing observation and thorough mapping of 
the lunar landscape. 
On July 22, 2019, ISRO launched the Chandrayaan -2 
mission, featuring the Optical High-Resolution Camera. The 
OHRC detects craters and boulders for two main purposes: 
ensuring safe lander deployment and supporting future 
scientific research [2]. Traditionally, crater detection involved 
manual visual inspection, which presents  challenges: (1) 
Identifying craters and boulders manually is time-consuming 
and complex. (2) Detecting the edges of very small or large 
craters can be difficult. 
The automated approaches overcome the limitations of 
traditional approaches with increased accuracy. However, 
over the years, a few approaches have been tested to overcome 
the above limitations. In [3], the authors described a  fully 
connected deep learning -based model for automatic crater 
detection. In [4], the authors described the automated lunar 
crater identification using deep learning. One notable 
limitation is that the model has not been specifically trained 
for boulder detection. Additionally, balancing the trade -off 
between how fast the system runs and how accurately it 
detects remains a major challenge.  
The paper introduces a novel approach for automatic crater 
and boulder detection that leverages the YOLOv11 model for 
high-resolution planetary imagery. This approach is trained, 
validated, and tested on the OHRC dataset [5]. The 
experimental analysis of the proposed approach is presented 
using YOLOv11 and RE-DETR [6]. The empirical study of 
this approach is conducted using key evaluation metrics, 
including Precision, Recall, mAP!"   and mAP!"#$! . Here, 
YOLOv11 outperforms RE -DETR for all the evaluation 
metrics. Hence, our further sections mainly discuss crater and 
boulder detection using the YOLOv11 model. 
The structure of the paper is outlined below.  Section II 
introduces the proposed YOLOv11-based crater and boulder 
detection approach. Section III details the setup and results of 
the experiment. Section IV completes the paper by providing 
a summary of the key findings and outlining potential 
directions for future work.

The dataset comprises 370 OHRC images in RGB format, each with a resolution of 1200 × 1200 pixels. The first step of the proposed approach is pre-processing of OHRC images, which includes resizing. Since the OHRC images vary in resolution and cannot be directly utilised, they are resized to a standardised resolution of 640 × 640 pixels to match YOLOv11’s input requirement. To enhance the robustness and generalizability of YOLOv11, classical data augmentation operations are applied(horizontal flip, vertical flip, 90-degree rotation) to increase the data. As a result, a total of 873 augmented images are generated, each with a resolution of 640 × 640 pixels. YOLOv11 is an upgraded version of the YOLOv8 model, designed to be more accurate and efficient [8]. It is made up of the backbone, neck and head. The main feature extractor is the backbone, which converts raw image data into multi-scale feature maps using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). It comprises stacked convolutional layers and specialised blocks designed to capture features at different scales. In the initial stage, OHRC images t%, t&,…, t'  are provided as input to the backbone, which processes them and generates a multi-scale feature map, denoted as FM(. The neck functions as a Feature Fusion layer, responsible for combining features from multiple scales through upsampling and concatenation. This step enhances the 
network’s ability to extract and integrate multi-scale information, improving detection accuracy across objects of different sizes. The backbone-generated feature map F( is upsampled to FM)* and concatenated to produce the fused feature map FM+,', ensuring that both fine-grained and high-level features contribute to the final predictions. CONCLUSION Using the YOLOv11 model, this research presents a novel approach for crater and boulder detection.  The dataset of OHRC images from ISRO's Bharatiya Antariksh Hackathon 2024 is used to analyse its effectiveness. Its results are compared to those of a RE-DETR-based crater and boulder 
detection approach. The YOLOv11-based model performed better in crater and boulder recognition than the RE-DETR-based model. During training, increases were reported in various metrics— precision, recall, mAP!", and mAP!"#$!—with gains of 4.8%, 2.4%, 5.0%, and 7.9% over the RE-DETR model. During the testing phase, the proposed approach attained an accuracy of 75.9%, demonstrating its reliability. Future work can explore strategies such as applying contrast enhancement techniques to better detect shallow craters. Morphological post-processing can be used to reduce noise and refine crater boundaries. Detailed error analysis can be performed using confusion matrices and class-wise metrics to guide model refinement.
